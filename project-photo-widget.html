01Why it exists

I wanted one picture on my home screen. Framed the way I’d frame it.

That’s really all this is: putting an image on my iPhone’s home screen. Apple’s own photo widget wouldn’t let me choose what it shows. I couldn’t give it just one picture — it had to be several — and in my opinion it just isn’t intuitive.

I didn’t want to go shopping on the App Store either. I just know some of those apps come with a payment, ads, or something else not to my liking.

So I made it myself. The first version, in June, did exactly one thing: put one photo of my choosing on the home screen.

In September I rebuilt it as a proper app. Now there can be several widgets, each with up to 50 photos that rotate on a schedule or shuffle, with filters, frames and captions. And it has no network code at all — nothing leaves the phone.

04What it does

Small app. No catch.

As many widgets as I like

Each widget on the home screen picks which one it shows from the Edit Widget menu, with thumbnails. One photo is still the simplest case; a widget can hold up to 50.

Smart Frame, then my hands

Photos arrive framed by Vision — faces first, then whatever stands out. From there: drag, pinch, double-tap, or a 1–5× slider, per photo and per size.

On a schedule

Every 15 or 30 minutes, hourly, every 3 or 6 hours, daily, or never — in order or shuffled. Tap the widget to move on to the next photo.

Filters, frames, captions

Ten Core Image filters with an intensity slider. Full Bleed, Border or Instant frames on six mats, two coloured from the photo. Captions: my own text, the date it was taken, or “time ago”, in five fonts.

Part of the system

A Shuffle Photos control in Control Center, a Siri shortcut, deep links, tinted Home Screen support, and an “On Home Screen” badge read from WidgetCenter.

Nothing leaves the phone

No StoreKit, no network code, no analytics — so no ads, no subscription and no account. The app says it plainly: “Your photos never leave your device.”

05How it’s built

The app does the work. The widget just shows it.

About 4,400 lines of Swift, designed for iOS 26’s Liquid Glass. It runs on my phone straight from Xcode — it isn’t on the App Store.

AppSwiftUI with Liquid Glass controls, Observation for state, PhotosUI for picking photos.
WidgetWidgetKit timelines, with App Intents for choosing a widget, tap-to-advance, the Control Center control and the Siri shortcut.
ImagesImageIO decodes, Vision frames, Core Image filters. Every photo is pre-rendered for every size into the shared App Group.
PipelineEdits re-render after a 350 ms pause, skip anything a render manifest says hasn’t changed, then reload the widget’s timelines. Writes are atomic, so the widget never reads half a file.
Tests12 Swift Testing tests: scheduling, shuffle fairness, crop maths and JSON round-trips.

06Build notes

The parts you don’t see.

01

A widget gets very little memory

Widget extensions run on a tight memory budget, so the heavy work — decoding, cropping, Lanczos scaling, filters — happens in the app, which pre-renders a finished JPEG for each photo and size. The widget only ever opens one small file, falling back to a thumbnail, then the photo’s average colour. Import decodes once, through an ImageIO thumbnail, to a 2,560 px master, a 540 px thumb and a 120 px blurred glow.

02

One crop that fits every shape

A crop is stored as a centre point and a zoom, not a fixed rectangle, so it projects onto a small square, a wide medium or a tall large — and changing the frame style never loses the framing. Vision picks the starting point.

03

App and widget agree without talking

Both work out the current photo with the same pure function: clock-aligned time slots plus a seeded shuffle (SplitMix64) that shows every photo once per cycle and never repeats one across a cycle boundary. It’s the code above.

04

A crop that feels right under a finger

Past the edges it rubber-bands on the same curve as UIScrollView. Near the middle it snaps to centre with a haptic tick, and pinch-zoom stays anchored under the fingers.
